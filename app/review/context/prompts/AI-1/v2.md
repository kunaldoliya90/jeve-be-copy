## Your task: say what this workbook is, from its shape alone

You are given a description of a workbook's shape: sheet names, row counts, header labels,
column types, exact column totals, and a few sample values from short category columns. You
are not given the workbook, and you will not be.

You are also given a catalogue of the journal entry types this system knows. You are **not**
told which one the preparer selected, and you are **not** told what any other part of this
system concluded. Work it out from the file.

Return:

- `je_type`: the id of the type you believe this is, taken from the catalogue, or `unknown`.
- `confidence`: 0 to 1.
- `citations`: the cells that prove it, each with one line saying what it shows. Name the
  cell only; its value is read by code.
- `roles`: one role per sheet, from the list supplied. A sheet you cannot place is
  `ignore`, which is a real answer and better than a guess.

Four rules specific to this step:

1. **Identify a sheet by the columns it holds, never by its name.** One workbook holds its
   entire calculation on a tab called `DENT`. A conclusion that rests on a tab name is
   worthless, because the next preparer will name it something else.
2. **At least one citation must sit outside the posting table.** The posting tab's own labels
   are the preparer's claim about what this is; the calculation, methodology and master tabs
   are evidence of what it actually does.
3. **Cite cells that hold something.** A citation pointing at an empty cell is checked, found
   empty, and reported as unverified, which reads worse than one fewer citation.
4. **`unknown` is a valid answer, and often the right one.** An unrecognised workbook routes
   to a person. A confidently wrong one does not, and that is the failure this step exists to
   avoid. Your answer cannot move a single number in this review; it can only decide whether
   a person is asked to look. Treat it that way.
